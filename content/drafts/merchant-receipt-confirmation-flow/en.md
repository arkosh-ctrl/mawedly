"She says she transferred the deposit, but I don't see anything in my bank account yet." A message arrives in the middle of a busy day, and you don't know: do you trust the client and hold the slot, or wait and risk upsetting someone who actually paid?

That is exactly the situation a clear bank-transfer confirmation system prevents — not by eliminating ambiguity entirely, but by making sure every party knows precisely where things stand at every moment.

Many business owners avoid bank transfer as a payment option specifically because of this scenario, assuming an automated payment gateway is the only fix for this ambiguity. The truth is the ambiguity isn't in the nature of bank transfers themselves — it's in the absence of a clear process for handling them, and that's solvable with simple steps, not something that requires abandoning bank transfer as an option entirely.

## The full mechanism, step by step

1. **The client picks a time and sees payment details immediately.** Bank name, IBAN or a QR code, and the amount due — all shown on the booking page itself, not in a separate message later.
2. **The client transfers the amount from their banking app.** Entirely outside the system — no payment gateway, no approval wait, no transaction fee.
3. **The client uploads a photo of the transfer receipt.** This step is what separates a clear system from simply "tell me you paid." A receipt is evidence, not a verbal claim.
4. **The booking shows "pending review," not "confirmed," immediately.** This is deliberate: neither the client nor the merchant should assume the booking is final before someone has actually checked the receipt.
5. **The merchant reviews the receipt and taps confirm or reject.** A simple visual check: is the amount correct? Is the date reasonable? Does the receipt belong to your account, not someone else's?
6. **The client receives a clear, final confirmation.** Only now does the booking move from "pending" to "confirmed and paid," and the client knows exactly that they can rely on their slot.

## Why "pending review" is necessary, not extra bureaucracy

Manual confirmation can look like a slow extra step compared to a payment gateway that verifies automatically within seconds. But this step is exactly what protects the merchant from a "but I paid and nobody registered it" dispute. Without review, any booking marked "confirmed" the moment a client claims to have paid opens the door to fake bookings or forged receipts. Review costs two minutes of your time in exchange for full confidence in every booking marked as paid.

This is also the core difference from payment gateways that take a cut of every transaction — the difference is not just cost, it is that manual review gives you direct visibility into every transaction, instead of full delegation to a third party.

## How long does review actually take

In practice, reviewing a clear receipt takes under a minute: open the image, check the amount and date, tap confirm. A good practical rule: review incoming receipts at least twice a day — morning and evening — instead of letting them pile up. A client waiting more than a few hours for confirmation starts to doubt how serious the business is, even if the delay was unintentional.

## What if the receipt is unclear or wrong

Do not leave the booking pending with no response. If the image is unclear or the amount does not match, reject the receipt with a message explaining why, and ask for a new upload or clarification. That is better than ignoring the booking or confirming it and hoping everything is correct.

## What if the client transfers more or less than required

This happens more than you would expect: a client transfers a rounded amount instead of the exact figure, or makes a typo entering the amount. The practical rule: never auto-confirm a booking when there is any discrepancy, however small. Reach out to the client first — "we noticed the transfer is $3 more than the required deposit — would you like it refunded, or applied to the service total?" That short exchange turns a potential mistake into extra trust, because the client sees you actually check every detail rather than just the receipt's general appearance.

A shortfall is more straightforward: an amount below the required deposit simply means the booking is not yet complete, and you tell the client exactly what remains instead of leaving them to guess why.

## Handling a high daily volume of receipts

If your business grows to the point where reviewing every single receipt takes real time, prioritise: review receipts tied to tomorrow's or the day after's appointments first — these are the most urgent, since the client is waiting for confirmation before their slot arrives. Receipts tied to appointments two weeks out can be reviewed during a quieter part of your day with no real downside.

## What if you're personally away for a full day

If you're the only person reviewing receipts, being away for a full day — travel, illness — means new booking confirmations stop entirely during that window. If you have a staff member you trust, consider giving them review access too, even in a limited way, to avoid that gap. If you run the business entirely solo, at least tell clients in advance through a general message that confirmations might be delayed a day or two during a specific period, instead of leaving the question unanswered.

## Your review record protects you too, not just the client

Every receipt you reviewed and confirmed or rejected stays a historical record protecting you in case of a later dispute. If a client later claims they paid and their booking was never confirmed, a clear record of when the receipt was rejected and why settles the discussion immediately, instead of relying on your memory alone weeks after the incident.
