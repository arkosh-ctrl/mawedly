A small consulting office booked "an appointment" at ten, and another at ten-thirty. It looked fine on the calendar — thirty minutes each, back to back, no visible conflict. The problem: the first appointment wasn't a quick consultation. It was a full file review that took an hour. The second client waited, then the third, and by noon the whole schedule was running a full hour late.

The problem here is not a one-off misjudgement. It is a structural error: treating "appointment" as one category, when it is actually two entirely different things in duration and nature.

This kind of mistake is hard to catch because it doesn't show up every day; it only shows up when long appointments happen to line up back-to-back on a given day, while other days pass with no visible problem simply because the mix happened to work out by chance, not by design.

## Why "appointment" is not one category in a professional office

A barbershop offers different services, but they are usually similar in structure. A professional office — law, financial advisory, real estate — usually offers two extremes: a short first consultation (could be 15-20 minutes, sometimes a free intro) and a long review or working session (an hour or more, requiring prior preparation from both sides). Treating both as one "appointment" with a uniform default duration is exactly what causes the collision later.

## How to categorise services in a way that prevents collisions

The fix is not padding every slot longer "just in case" — that wastes real time on ordinary days. The fix is categorising each service type by its actual duration from the start: first consultation = 20 minutes, case review = 60 minutes, follow-up session = 30 minutes. When a client books, they pick the service type first, and the system reserves the correct duration automatically instead of one default that fits nothing precisely.

This is a general principle covered from the team angle in [the article on scheduling a multi-provider team](/en/blog/multi-provider-scheduling-conflicts), but here it concerns the variety of the service itself, not the number of providers offering it.

## First consultation vs. follow-up session: why separating them from day one pays off

Splitting the two types has a second benefit beyond preventing delays: it lets you price each differently. A short first consultation might be free or token-priced to attract new clients; a long follow-up session deserves the full price and perhaps a larger deposit, because its time is more valuable and harder to recover if cancelled last minute. Separating the two from the start makes that pricing distinction natural, not a manual exception every time.

## Buffer time between sessions: the detail everyone forgets

Even with precise service categorisation, a session ending exactly at 11:00 followed by another starting exactly at 11:00 assumes you move from one file to the next with zero gap — no time to write notes, catch your breath, or absorb a small delay from the previous client. Add a short buffer (five to ten minutes) specifically between longer sessions, counted as part of the service's own duration rather than "empty" calendar time. This prevents delays from accumulating throughout the day from an entirely different source than service categorisation.

## When a first session should be paid, not free

Some offices offer a free first consultation to attract new clients, and that's reasonable in early stages. But if free calls attract a large share of the curious rather than the committed — as covered in [the article on the free consultation nobody shows up to](/en/blog/free-consultation-no-shows) — switching to a small token fee might solve both the attendance and the scheduling problem at once.

## Why "playing it safe" on duration creates its own problem

The obvious counter-fix to collisions might seem simple: pad every appointment with extra time just in case. But that creates the opposite problem — a normal day with no delays leaves the office sitting idle for unused hours, because every appointment is booked for longer than it actually needs most of the time. Precise categorisation by actual service type beats a general safety margin applied indiscriminately to everything.

## A pre-session intake form for longer sessions

For long sessions specifically — a case review, comprehensive financial planning — consider a short form the client fills out a day or two before the appointment: what's the main topic? What relevant documents are there? This doesn't necessarily shorten the session itself, but it makes it more focused from the first minute, instead of spending the first quarter-hour gathering context that could have been collected in advance.

## Short follow-ups: don't treat them as a full consultation

A client returning with a quick question after a main session doesn't need a full appointment at the same length as the first consultation. A third category sometimes deserves its own classification: a "quick follow-up" at 10-15 minutes, entirely separate from the first consultation and the long review. This prevents booking more time than a simple question needs, and frees that extra time for another client who genuinely needs it.

## A client needing longer than usual: handling it without breaking the system

Sometimes you know in advance that a particular client's file is exceptionally complex and needs more time than the standard category. Don't break the system with a manual booking outside the classification; add a new dedicated category if this pattern repeats ("complex review" with a longer duration than "standard review"), or for a rare case, book two consecutive slots clearly instead of manually extending one in a way that might confuse the system later.
