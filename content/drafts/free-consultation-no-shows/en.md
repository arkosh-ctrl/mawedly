Three discovery calls booked this week. One person turned up. The second apologised ten minutes before. The third sent nothing at all.

Then comes the wrong question: "Is my pricing off? Is my profile unconvincing? Is the timing bad?"

The right question is different: **what did the person who skipped it actually lose?** Nothing. That is why they skipped it.

## Free appointments do not occupy calendar space

A free appointment never enters the day's arithmetic. When a client meeting and a free intro call both land at eleven, there is no decision to make. The paid meeting wins automatically and the free call is dropped without guilt.

That is not disrespect. It is ordinary prioritisation. People rank commitments by cost, and a cost of zero produces a priority of zero.

This is not just an impression. In a 2007 *Marketing Science* paper, [Shampanier, Mazar and Ariely](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf) showed that zero is not simply a very low price but a category the mind treats differently: people systematically overvalue free options when choosing between them. The flip side of that bias is what you see in your calendar — a decision made without any real trade-off, and therefore just as easy to reverse.

The consequence is that free calls attract two types of people:

- **The curious.** They want to see how you think, or get a quick answer they can act on themselves. They will not buy, and they never intended to.
- **The very undecided.** Genuinely interested, but far from a purchase decision. For them the call is exploration, not commitment.

Both cost you more than you think: thirty minutes on the call, fifteen preparing, fifteen afterwards writing notes or following up. A full hour per call, attended or not.

## Price what you are actually paying

Take a sheet of paper and run it on last month, not on a general impression:

1. How many discovery calls did you book?
2. How many were no-shows?
3. How many happened but were never going to convert — and you knew that within five minutes?
4. Multiply (2 + 3) by one hour, then by your consulting hourly rate.

That is not money "lost" in an accounting sense, but it is time that could have gone to a paying client, to sharpening your offer, or to rest. Your time is the only inventory you sell, and you sold part of it for nothing.

## Three fixes, lightest to strongest

### 1. Qualify before the call with three short questions

Before confirming any slot, ask for three written answers:

- What problem are you trying to solve right now?
- What have you already tried?
- What is your rough timeline and budget?

Anyone who answers all three has invested five minutes before meeting you. That small investment does two jobs: it raises the odds they attend, and it tells you in advance whether the call is worth taking.

And the ones who do not answer? You lost nothing. You avoided an hour that was going to evaporate.

### 2. Make the call shorter and more specific

"30-minute intro call" is a vague title that promises nothing in particular, and vague things are easy to cancel.

Replace it with a defined promise: "15 minutes to decide whether I am the right person for your situation — and if I am not, to point you in the right direction." A specific promise is harder to cancel because it sounds useful on its own, rather than a preamble to a pitch.

### 3. Charge a small fee — this is where it changes

This is the fix most consultants resist, and the one with the largest effect.

A modest fee for the first call does four things at once:

- **Attendance climbs sharply**, because people who pay, show up.
- **The merely curious filter themselves out**, with no confrontation and no awkward decline from you.
- **Your position shifts.** You become an expert who is consulted for a fee, not a vendor asking for a chance to present.
- **The relationship starts on commercial footing**, so discussing price later is a normal conversation rather than a surprise.

The predictable objection: "I'll get fewer calls." Yes — that is the point. Ten free calls where five attend and one converts, against four paid calls where four attend and two convert. Fewer calls, better outcome, and the remaining hours come back to you.

If the step feels too large, make the fee **creditable against the first engagement**. Then a serious client pays nothing extra at all, and a non-serious one pays for your time. That is the fairest structure I have found.

## What about referrals and past clients

Exempt anyone who arrives through a client referral, and anyone you have worked with before. They have demonstrated seriousness another way, and their attendance rate is already high. The rule is aimed at the stranger arriving from the internet, not at your network.
