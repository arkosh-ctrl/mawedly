"Can we move today's lesson to tomorrow? Something came up."

You reply: "Of course, no problem." Then you open the schedule and find tomorrow is full, so you shift another lesson, apologise to a third family, and finish the week having rebuilt it twice.

Nothing was cancelled. Nothing was deducted from your income — in theory. And yet you worked more and earned less. The feeling is accurate. The reason is obvious once you look, and invisible in every calculation.

## Rescheduling looks free because it hides its cost

A cancellation is honest. A lesson is gone, the loss is visible, everyone notices.

Rescheduling is quieter. The lesson still "exists", it simply moved, so it appears nobody lost anything. In reality it imposes four hidden costs:

- **Rebuilding the schedule.** Every reschedule shifts one or two other lessons, and every shift needs calls, apologies and confirmations.
- **A slot that cannot be sold.** The original hour was blocked, so you turned another student away. Now it is empty with hours' notice and unsellable.
- **Fragmented attention.** Teaching needs preparation and presence. A schedule in constant motion burns energy that was supposed to go into the lesson itself.
- **Broken learning rhythm.** A student on a regular schedule progresses. A student who studies whenever it is convenient spends each session recovering what they forgot — and the tutor gets blamed for slow results.

The fourth cost is the professionally dangerous one, because it damages your reputation rather than your calendar.

## Run it on your own month

Take last month:

1. How many lessons were rescheduled?
2. On average, how many minutes did each reschedule take — messages, calls, calendar edits?
3. Multiply, and convert to hours.
4. Multiply the hours by your lesson rate.

That is unpaid administrative work you genuinely performed. It is usually larger than expected, because each reschedule feels trivial in isolation.

## Why parents reschedule so easily

Because it does not feel like a big request — and honestly, from where they stand, it is not:

- **No financial commitment is attached to the time.** Payment usually comes after the lesson or at month end, so the slot itself carries no value in their mental arithmetic.
- **They cannot see your schedule.** They do not know you declined another student for that hour, or that moving their lesson moves three others.
- **Precedent becomes policy.** The first reschedule you accepted with a smile established an unspoken rule: this tutor is flexible. Unlimited flexibility gets consumed.
- **The lesson ranks last.** Between a family event and a maths lesson, the lesson always moves — because it is the only thing that can.

Note that the parent is not acting in bad faith in any of these cases. They are behaving rationally inside a system where you never set an edge.

## The system that breaks the loop

### 1. Sell a package, not a lesson

A single lesson is easy to buy and easy to move. A package — say eight lessons — changes the frame entirely: the family bought a programme, and a programme has a start, an end and a rhythm.

Packages give you three things: income known in advance, real commitment from the family, and the consistency that makes progress visible.

### 2. Write one clear rescheduling rule

Do not ban rescheduling. That is unrealistic and life happens. Bound it instead:

> "One lesson per package may be rescheduled, with at least 12 hours' notice. Later changes or no-shows count as a lesson from the package."

That sentence does three jobs: it acknowledges that circumstances occur, it sets a countable limit, and it attaches a real cost to late changes. Most importantly it is written down, so you never have to have the conversation personally.

### 3. Present the terms before the first lesson, not after the first problem

A rule stated after the third reschedule sounds like a complaint. The same rule presented alongside the package details before you start sounds like professional structure. Identical text, entirely different reception.

### 4. Fix weekly slots

"Every Sunday and Tuesday at 6" is far stronger than "we'll agree each time". A fixed slot enters the family's routine and becomes part of the week. A slot negotiated each time stays negotiable each time.

### 5. Send one reminder the day before

A single message 24 hours out prevents most last-minute reschedules, because it gives the family a chance to spot the clash early instead of an hour beforehand.

## What changes after a month

Tutors who apply this consistently report three shifts:

- The number of reschedules falls on its own, because families start planning rather than assuming flexibility.
- Income becomes predictable, so you can plan your own month.
- The relationship with families improves rather than sours, because clarity removes mutual awkwardness.

That last point surprises people. Written boundaries do not repel serious parents. They reassure them: this tutor is organised, values their own time, and probably values my child's time too.
