They cancelled Wednesday's session two hours beforehand. You told yourself: "They're going through a difficult time, I can't charge for that." It happened three times in two months. On the fourth, you felt something you could not quite name — and then felt guilty for feeling it.

Anyone working in counselling or family therapy recognises this. It is not purely an administrative problem. It touches the work itself.

## A consistent frame is not the opposite of compassion

In the helping professions, the frame — a fixed time, a defined length, a clear policy — is not an organisational detail sitting outside the session. It is part of the working environment, a principle long established in counselling practice.

A session that starts on time and ends on time, under terms both people knew in advance, produces three effects:

- **Predictability.** A client who knows exactly when they will see you and what happens if they cannot attend is relating to a defined space rather than a relationship with unclear edges.
- **Consistency.** A rule applied to everyone in the same way generates no comparisons and no sense of arbitrary treatment.
- **Continuity.** Regular attendance is what allows sessions to accumulate instead of restarting each time.

Undeclared leniency produces the opposite of what we assume. Every cancellation becomes a fresh negotiation, and every negotiation loads the relationship with something that does not belong to it.

## What happens to the practitioner without a policy

The side that is discussed far too rarely:

- **Unpredictable income.** Three cancelled sessions in a week means a week without earnings, on time you kept free.
- **Quiet resentment.** A practitioner who repeatedly waives fees begins to feel resentment towards the person they are waiving them for — and that is the most damaging thing that can enter the room.
- **Professional exhaustion.** Absent boundaries are a well-recognised contributor to burnout in helping professions, and burnout degrades the quality of the work before it affects anything else.
- **Time that cannot be recovered.** The hour you held open cannot be resold, and you may have declined someone else who was waiting for it.

Clarity is not severity towards clients. It is a condition of your ability to keep doing good work.

## How to write the policy

Three sentences, presented before the first session rather than after the first cancellation:

> "Sessions are 50 minutes, at a fixed weekly time. Cancellation or rescheduling with more than 24 hours' notice carries no charge. Later cancellations or non-attendance without notice are charged as a full session."

Then add a fourth sentence that makes the difference:

> "In a genuine emergency, contact me and we will find a way."

That sentence matters for two reasons. First, emergencies genuinely occur, and a policy that does not acknowledge them is not a realistic policy. Second, it converts the rule from a wall into a frame: firm as standard, flexible when it must be.

Note the distinction. A declared exception remains an exception. Undeclared leniency becomes the rule within two months.

## Presentation: timing is everything

The same policy is read in two entirely different ways depending on when it appears:

- **Before the first session:** it reads as professional structure, and the client agrees to it from a settled position.
- **After the first missed session:** it reads as reproach, and puts the working relationship on the defensive.

Make it part of the first confirmation message, alongside length, fee and format. Two lines in a confirmation replace ten awkward conversations later.

## The privacy question

In this field, privacy is not an added benefit. It is a baseline requirement. Three practical points:

- **Never name the service in any notification.** "Your appointment is tomorrow at 6pm" is entirely sufficient. There is no need to state the type of session in text that may appear on a locked screen.
- **Separate appointment data from session notes.** A booking system needs a name, a number and a time. Clinical notes belong somewhere else, protected separately.
- **Think about the payment route.** Some clients prefer that no clinic or centre name appears on their bank statement. A direct bank transfer gives the client more control over this than a payment gateway does.

## What about clients who genuinely cannot pay

This is a real issue with a well-known professional answer: allocate a limited number of reduced-fee or free places, and state the arrangement openly in advance.

The distinction is fundamental. A declared reduced-fee place is a professional decision you made. Repeated undeclared leniency is a decision made on your behalf, and it drains you gradually with no defined end.
