"Nobody told me the deposit wasn't refundable." A sentence you hear after a dispute with an upset client, and when you check back through the booking conversation, the refund term genuinely was never stated clearly — it was said verbally, or assumed to be understood. The dispute was not caused by a bad policy. It was caused by the absence of a written one both sides could point to.

## Why a written policy protects both sides, not just the merchant

The common assumption is that a cancellation policy exists to protect the merchant from the client. That is only half the picture. A clear policy protects the client too, from ambiguity: they know exactly what happens if they need to cancel, instead of relying on the merchant's judgment case by case. That clarity is what prevents most disputes in the first place — not because everyone agrees with every detail, but because each side knows the rule in advance.

## Five elements no policy is complete without

1. **When the amount is fully refunded.** Usually a cancellation before a set window (24 or 48 hours, for example).
2. **When it is not.** A cancellation too close to the appointment, or a no-show with zero notice.
3. **How cancellation actually happens.** A direct link, a message, a call — name the channel clearly.
4. **What happens if the business itself needs to reschedule** (an emergency day off, a circumstance on your end) — the client deserves the same clarity in the reverse direction.
5. **Where the policy appears.** It needs to be visible before the booking is confirmed, not in a message that arrives after payment.

## A ready template you can adapt

> **Cancellation and Refund Policy**
> The deposit is fully refunded if cancelled at least 24 hours before the appointment. Cancelling after that window, or not showing up with no notice, means the deposit is not refunded. To cancel or change your appointment, use the link in your confirmation message. If we need to reschedule your appointment for any reason on our end, we will let you know as soon as possible and offer either an alternative time or a full refund.

This is a baseline that fits most businesses, and the window can be adjusted (24 hours might become 48 for longer services), or an exception added for documented emergencies if you want one.

## Where to place this text

Most of this policy's impact comes from when it is shown, not just how it is worded. As covered in [the article on deposits as a commitment tool](/en/blog/deposit-as-commitment-not-punishment), a policy shown before a booking is confirmed reads as a fair term the client agreed to themselves; the same policy sent after a no-show reads as a surprise penalty, even if the text is word-for-word identical.

## Important note: this is not legal advice

This template is a practical starting point, not an approved legal document. Commercial regulations and consumer rights differ between Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Kuwait, and can change. If your business handles large sums or faces frequent disputes, consult a local lawyer or the relevant official authority — in Saudi Arabia, for instance, the [Ministry of Commerce](https://mc.gov.sa) is the reference body for consumer rights.

## Two real-world examples to show how it applies

**Case one:** a client cancels a colour appointment 30 hours ahead. The policy states a full refund for cancellations over 24 hours out, the condition is clearly met, and the refund is automatic with no discussion.

**Case two:** a client booked a consulting session, then messaged ten minutes after it started to apologise for not coming. No room for interpretation here: this is a no-show, plain and simple, and the written policy settles it with no need for a judgment call in the moment.

The difference between the two cases is that both are settled by the pre-written text, not by the mood of the moment or the relationship with the client. That is exactly what a clear policy gives you: a fast decision with no tension, every time.

## Revise the policy if the exception keeps repeating

If you find yourself making the same exception repeatedly — say, accepting a 12-hour cancellation despite the policy stating 24 — that's a sign the policy itself doesn't reflect your business's reality, not that clients are gaming it. Revise the text to match what you actually enforce, instead of keeping a written rule neither side actually follows.

## Why a general template beats silence, even before a lawyer reviews it

Some business owners delay writing any policy because they're waiting for the "correct legal wording" first. That wait is unnecessary: a simple, clear policy written today is far better than nothing for months while a perfect version gets drafted. You can start with the template above, and revise it later after legal advice if you actually need it — gradual clarity beats delayed perfection.

## One unified policy, or different ones per service?

A single default rule is simpler for a client to understand and for you to manage. But some businesses genuinely need an exception for one specific service — an event photography booking, say, might deserve a longer cancellation window than 24 hours (a week, perhaps), because replacing that date is far harder than replacing a normal salon hour. If you have one or two services that differ fundamentally from the rest in how hard they are to resell, give them a clearly written special policy right underneath, instead of forcing them into a general rule that doesn't fit.

## Review the policy at least once a year

Your business changes — new services, different prices, a bigger team — and the written policy needs to keep up with that instead of staying frozen from day one. Set aside time at least once a year to read your current policy and confirm it still reflects how you actually run your business, not how you ran it when you first wrote it.

## Keep the tone human, not dry legalese

A policy written in dense legal language repels rather than reassures. The template suggested above is deliberately written in short, direct sentences, not "party of the first part" terminology. A good policy reads like a person explaining a fair rule, not a legal document meant to intimidate an ordinary reader.
