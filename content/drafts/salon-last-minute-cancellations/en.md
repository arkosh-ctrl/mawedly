The message arrives at eleven in the morning. "So sorry, I can't make it today." The appointment was at one — colour and lift, three hours blocked for one stylist.

You now have two hours to sell three. It will not sell.

This is not an ordinary cancellation problem, and misreading it is why most salons respond to it in the wrong way.

## The problem is block length, not cancellation rate

A salon taking thirty bookings a week and losing three looks healthy: a ten percent cancellation rate. The number lies, because it treats every appointment as equal.

A thirty-minute cut cancelled an hour ahead? You can often fill it from a waiting list or a walk-in. Reasonable odds.

A three-hour colour cancelled an hour ahead? You need someone who is free right now, wants a long service specifically, can reach you within the hour, and is willing to commit to a significant spend on impulse. That person exists in theory. You cannot summon her.

**The rule:** the longer the service, the lower the chance of reselling it, and the higher the cost of a late cancellation.

Which means a salon losing two long appointments a week is bleeding more than one losing six short ones — even though the second looks worse in a percentage.

## Run the number on your own salon

You do not need accounting software. You need one sheet of paper.

1. List your long services — anything over two hours — with prices.
2. Count how many of those were cancelled late or missed entirely last month.
3. Multiply the count by the average price.
4. Add the stylist's wage for those hours if she is on a fixed salary. That is paid whether she works or waits.

Multiply the result by twelve. That is what late cancellations cost you annually on long services alone.

Most owners running this calculation for the first time find the figure lands somewhere near a new piece of equipment or an extra salary.

## Why clients cancel at the last minute

The question deserves an honest answer before any fix:

- **Booking cost them nothing.** They booked two weeks ago with a one-line message. Cancelling carries the identical cost: another one-line message.
- **Real life.** A family emergency, a sick child, a day that fell apart. This happens and cannot be prevented — but it can reach you early instead of at the last hour.
- **Parallel booking.** They held a slot with you and with another salon, then decided.
- **Avoidance.** They changed their mind about the service or the price, and found that apologising an hour ahead is easier than apologising a week ahead, because nobody argues with you at the last minute.

Only the second reason deserves full forgiveness. The other three exist because booking is entirely free, and free things are not respected.

## The deposit: one sentence decides whether it works

Plenty of salons have tried asking for a deposit and abandoned it after the first objection. Usually the idea was not the problem. The wording was.

Compare two ways of saying the same thing:

> "You have to pay 50 riyals upfront to hold the appointment, and it's non-refundable if you don't come."

> "Your appointment is three hours, reserved for you alone. We take 50 riyals of the service price upfront and deduct it from your final bill. If you need to cancel more than 24 hours ahead, it comes back in full."

The second version says four things the first does not:

- **The money is part of the price, not an extra fee.** She will not pay one riyal more.
- **The time is held exclusively for her.** It justifies the request instead of imposing it.
- **The refund is guaranteed under a clear condition.** It removes the fear of losing money.
- **The window is specific.** Twenty-four hours is a rule someone can follow. "Early" is not.

Apply deposits to long, high-value services first — colour, keratin, protein treatments, bridal — and leave short services alone. That way you keep the walk-in client and protect the blocks you cannot replace.

## Three supporting habits

### An actual waiting list

Every late cancellation should trigger one question: who did we turn away this week? If you keep the names of clients who asked for unavailable slots, a cancellation turns from a certain loss into an opportunity. Without a written list, that is just a nice idea.

### One confirmation, 24 hours out

A single message the day before, framing cancellation as a dignified option: "Your appointment is tomorrow at 1 — if that no longer works, tell us now and we'll offer it to someone else." A cancellation a day ahead is a win, because you still have time to resell.

### A written policy shown before confirmation

Two sentences the client sees before she confirms, not after she misses. Same text, different timing — and timing is what turns a penalty into an agreement.
