"We do remind the client, and they still miss it." That complaint comes from business owners who already send a reminder — just at the wrong time, or worded in a way that prompts no action. A reminder is not a box you tick by sending something; its timing and wording decide whether it works.

## What the data says about timing

Automated SMS and email reminders cut no-shows by up to 50%, according to [SchedulingKit](https://schedulingkit.com/statistics/no-show-statistics)'s aggregation citing a Cochrane systematic review. A separate study published via [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30612033/) found that a phone reminder alone cut the no-show rate from 20.99% to 7.07% — a direct, not hypothetical, effect.

Timing specifically has a measured role: the same aggregation reports a 29% reduction in no-shows when reminders go out 24 to 48 hours ahead specifically — not earlier, not closer. This lines up with simple logic: a week ahead, the appointment is too distant to stay top of mind. An hour ahead, there is no longer enough time to rearrange if something conflicts. The 24-to-48-hour window is what leaves real room to act.

## Is text better than a call or email?

Per the same data, 80% of SMS reminders are read within five minutes of arrival. But that does not mean every channel fits every business: some systems — Mawedly included — use email specifically instead of WhatsApp or SMS, for a practical reason: email is archivable and searchable, does not get buried in a long chat thread, and arrives without depending on a third-party app that could change its policies on business messaging. The difference is not speed — it is that email stays a reference the client can return to, while a WhatsApp message quickly sinks under other conversations.

## Why "more" is not always better

It is tempting to assume an extra reminder always helps. But [Tebra](https://www.tebra.com/theintake/patient-experience/patient-scheduling-retention/ways-to-reduce-no-shows-cancellations-healthcare)'s survey found a clear split by age group: 40% of patients overall said providers should send more reminders, rising to roughly 50% among Gen Z and 45% among Millennials. In other words: a younger client often prefers multiple reminders, while an older one may find frequent reminders annoying.

There is no single rule that fits everyone, but a simple practical guideline usually works: one reminder 24-48 hours ahead is enough for routine services, and two — one the day before, one a few hours ahead — for high-value appointments or a new client who has not yet built an attendance habit.

## Wording matters more than frequency

"Reminder: you have an appointment Thursday" is administrative and cold and prompts no action. Compare:

> ❌ "Reminder: you have an appointment Thursday at 5."

> ✅ "Your appointment is Thursday at 5 with [provider name]. If that no longer works, cancel here now and we'll offer the slot to someone else."

The difference is not just tone. The second version does two things: it restates the full details (no need to dig up the original booking message), and it opens a clear, easy path to early cancellation instead of silent absence. An early cancellation is a win for you — it returns the slot to the market while it can still be resold.

## When you need a second confirmation channel

Some appointments — a long service, or a first-time new client — deserve an explicit confirmation request, not just a notice. A message asking "is this time still good for you?" that expects a reply lets you catch a likely no-show a full day ahead, instead of discovering it in the moment, when it is already too late to resell the slot.

## How to know your reminder is actually working

Don't assume a change in timing or wording worked just because it sounds logical. Compare two numbers, before and after: the no-show rate over two weeks before you changed the reminder, and the same rate over two weeks after. If nothing moved, the problem may not be the reminder at all — it may be something else that needs a different fix, such as the absence of a deposit on longer services, as covered in [the article on deposits as a commitment tool](/en/blog/deposit-as-commitment-not-punishment).

Measuring this does not require a complex tool; a two-column paper log is enough: number of appointments, and number of no-shows with no notice. Compare the two periods honestly, not against a vague sense that "things feel a bit better."

## In the same language as the booking

As with the initial confirmation message, a later reminder in a different language than the one the client booked in signals a lack of attention to detail. As covered in [the article on wording a confirmation message](/en/blog/booking-confirmation-message-wording), every message's language — confirmation or reminder — should follow the language of the specific page the client used, not one default setting applied to everyone.

## A special reminder for a new client's first appointment

A first-time client doesn't yet have the "habit" of showing up with you, unlike a regular used to your schedule. An extra, slightly earlier reminder — or more detailed wording explaining how to get there or what to bring — reduces the odds of a no-show specifically on that first visit, even if you don't apply the same level of detail to your regulars.

## Don't forget to remind your team too

Everything discussed above is aimed at the client, but your team sometimes needs a similar reminder — especially for an appointment added last minute or one whose time changed. A system that notifies the provider themselves about their upcoming appointment, not just the client, reduces the odds that the absent party this time turns out to be the business itself, not the client — a rare scenario, but a particularly embarrassing one when it happens.

## Don't send the reminder as a warning

A subtle wording distinction worth flagging: a reminder aims to help, not to imply a threat. Phrases like "please arrive on time or your booking will be cancelled" create a defensive feeling in the reader even if they already intended to show up. Keeping a warm, confident tone, even when mentioning cancellation terms, makes the reminder read as a service rather than a warning.
