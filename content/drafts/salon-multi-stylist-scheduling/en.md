A salon owner with four stylists compared three booking systems before choosing one. Two priced "per seat" — meaning the monthly price quadrupled because she has four staff, not because the salon itself is bigger or more complex. The third priced the business as one unit, regardless of how many stylists worked inside it.

That difference in pricing model sounds like an administrative detail, but it actually decides how much you pay monthly for exactly the same need: coordinating four people's schedules without conflicts.

The problem is that this detail rarely shows up clearly on any booking system's homepage. The advertised price is usually "starting from" an attractive small number, while the actual details of what happens when you add a second or third staff member only appear on the full pricing page, or sometimes only after direct contact with a sales team.

## Per seat vs. per business: the difference in numbers

The "per seat" model is common in larger salon management software, and it has a logic: every added staff member means genuinely more system usage. But in practice, it means a salon with four stylists pays four times what a one-stylist salon pays, even if their monthly booking volume is relatively close given comparable working hours.

The "per business" model flips that: one price covers a reasonable number of providers, so adding a fifth stylist becomes a purely operational decision, not one that automatically triggers a bigger subscription bill.

Neither model is universally "correct" — it depends on your salon's size and growth plans. But it deserves an explicit comparison before you choose, not an assumption that the price shown on the website is what you will actually pay once your full team is added.

## Scheduling conflicts multiply with every added stylist

As covered in more detail in [the article on scheduling a multi-provider team](/en/blog/multi-provider-scheduling-conflicts), the odds of a conflict between two bookings depend not just on the number of stylists, but on the number of booking channels used too. Four stylists whose clients book by phone, WhatsApp, and walk-in carry a much higher conflict risk than one stylist with a single booking channel. The fix is not "pay closer attention" — it is one unified calendar where everyone, you and every stylist, sees every booking the moment it happens.

## Who manages whose schedule

A practical question often overlooked: does each stylist need to see only their own schedule, or everyone's? The usual best answer: each stylist manages her own bookings and working hours, while you as the salon owner see the full team picture from one place. This split prevents "who edited what" confusion, and keeps each stylist's schedule private from the others if that is what you want.

## Senior vs. newer stylists: different pricing and duration

Not every stylist is equal in price or experience, and that should be reflected in the system rather than managed manually outside it. A senior stylist might charge more for the exact same service than a newer one, and might take less time thanks to experience. A system that ties price and duration to the specific provider, not just the general service, saves you from uniform pricing that doesn't reflect the real difference in value delivered.

## Walk-ins with no prior booking: handling them without breaking the system

Some salons take walk-in clients, especially for short services. If your unified calendar only reflects bookings, a walk-in client might "collide" with a slot already booked online without anyone realising. The fix: designate short slots explicitly marked "walk-in only" per stylist if you want to keep that option, instead of leaving it to in-the-moment guessing.

## Stylist commission: outside the booking system, but connected to it

Some salons pay stylists a percentage of each service rather than a fixed salary. That's not directly a booking-system matter, but a clear record of every booking tied to its provider makes calculating those commissions later far easier than trying to reconstruct them from memory or a scattered notebook at month's end.

## Onboarding a new stylist to the system

When a new stylist joins the team, the first two weeks are critical for building the right habit with the system. Don't assume she'll figure everything out alone; set aside half an hour to walk through adding her working hours, handling a booking that needs an edit, and finding her daily schedule. That small investment prevents bigger mistakes later — like entering the wrong working hours, which opens the door to bookings during a time she isn't actually there.

## Annual leave: plan it in the system, not after the fact

A stylist's planned annual leave should go into her schedule the moment the dates are set, not the day before it starts. That prevents bookings mistakenly accepted during a known absence period, and gives your team enough time to redistribute any existing bookings within that window to another stylist instead of cancelling them at the last minute.

## Compare at least three systems before deciding

The decision between "per seat" and "per business" pricing deserves an actual comparison, not picking the first system you find. Ask for a written quote from at least three different systems, assuming your team grows to six or seven within two years, and calculate the total expected cost of each option over that period, not just the current monthly price.
