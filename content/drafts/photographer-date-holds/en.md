"We'd like to book you for the 18th — we'll confirm in about a week."

You pencil the date into your calendar and wait. A week passes; you follow up and get "we haven't finalised things yet." Two weeks pass. Then another client asks about the same date and you decline, because it is "sort of taken". In the end the first client never confirms, and the second has already found another photographer.

You traded confirmed work for a possibility that never materialised.

## A date is not an appointment. It is scarce inventory

What separates event work from most service businesses is that your inventory is limited both in number and by season:

- Weekends carry the overwhelming majority of events.
- Demand is concentrated: holidays, school breaks, wedding season.
- Every date sells exactly once and never returns.

If you have thirty workable Saturdays in a year, you are not selling "appointments" — you are selling thirty scarce units. Freezing one without commitment means immobilising three percent of your annual capacity on the strength of a phone call.

## Price your held dates

From last year's calendar:

1. How many dates did you provisionally hold that never converted?
2. Of those, how many involved turning another client away?
3. Multiply the second number by your average package value.

That is a real cash loss, not a hypothetical one. Someone was ready to pay and you declined them.

Then add a line that never appears anywhere: every client you turned away who went to another photographer and liked the work became that photographer's client permanently — and will recommend them, not you.

## Why clients ask for a hold without committing

Usually without any bad intent:

- **The decision is not theirs alone.** They are waiting on family, on the venue, on a relative travelling in.
- **They are comparing quotes.** They want to secure your option while they finish comparing, and a free hold costs them nothing.
- **They assume the hold is non-binding for you too.** They cannot imagine you declining work because of it, since they cannot see your calendar.
- **They are deferring the financial decision.** Paying makes the event real; postponing keeps it comfortable.

Only the first reason is fully legitimate, and it has a solution that does not cost you anything. A deposit handles the rest.

## The rule: a date is held by deposit only

This is not harshness. It is the professional norm in event photography worldwide. What determines how it lands is the wording.

Compare:

> "I can't hold a date without a deposit."

> "The 18th is available right now. I'll lock it under your name as soon as a 20% deposit arrives, and it comes off the total. Until then the date stays open to anyone — and I'll tell you immediately if someone else asks for it, so you can decide."

The second version says four things:

- **The condition is explicit, with a number.** Nothing to misread.
- **The deposit is part of the price, not an extra fee.**
- **The current status is transparent:** the date is not held, so there is no surprise later.
- **You promised to warn them before selling it.** That sentence in particular turns a condition into a service, and creates gentle urgency without pressure.

## An option for clients waiting on a family decision

Offer a paid temporary hold: a small non-refundable amount that reserves the date for seven days and is credited towards the deposit if they proceed.

This is fair on both sides. They buy real time; you are paid for immobilising scarce inventory. And anyone unwilling to pay a token amount for a week was never close to booking.

## Three practices that complete the picture

### Write what the price includes before they ask

Much of the delay comes from a vague quote. One page covering hours of coverage, number of edited images, delivery time, and what counts as an extra. Clarity accelerates a decision more than any follow-up message.

### Send reminders a week and a day before

With large events, people forget the details rather than the date: arrival time, meeting point, who the contact person is. Two short messages prevent most of the confusion on the day.

### Keep the cancellation policy written and short

Two sentences: what is refundable and when, and what happens if the event is cancelled at short notice. Presented with the quote, not after the problem.
